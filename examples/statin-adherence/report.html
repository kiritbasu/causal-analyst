How much does sticking to your statin reduce cardiac hospital admissions over the next year?

The best estimate is about 4.5 fewer cardiac admissions per 100 members a year (range 2.8 to 6.2), but that figure is very likely too high. Adherent members also had about 20% fewer injury admissions, which a statin cannot cause, so part of the gap is people who look after themselves generally. A rough correction for that brings it to about 2 per 100, in line with trial evidence, and that corrected figure is still consistent with no effect. For the business case, use about 2 per 100 as the planning number and 4.5 as a ceiling.

What usually goes wrong with this question

We wrote these down from published evidence before looking at any results. They are general knowledge, not confirmed by you.

Healthy-adherer effectgeneral knowledge, not confirmed
People who take their medicines as prescribed also tend to exercise, eat better, keep appointments and avoid risks, so they have fewer admissions of every kind, whether or not the pill works. Classic evidence: adherent statin users also had fewer road accidents and workplace injuries (Dormuth et al., Circulation 2009), and people who took their placebo faithfully in trials lived longer.
What we did: Prior flu vaccination used as a partial stand-in control; accidental-injury admissions used as a negative-control outcome; the unrecorded part is drawn in the diagram and sized in the hidden-factor chart.
Sick-stopper / reverse causationgeneral knowledge, not confirmed
Members who become frail or ill early on may stop filling prescriptions, and are also more likely to be admitted, which makes non-adherence look harmful.
What we did: Prior-year admissions and baseline risk factors are controlled; the 6-month adherence window ends before the 12-month outcome window starts, which avoids counting early events. Remaining risk noted as a question.
Adherence is not the programmegeneral knowledge, not confirmed
Finance's number is for a programme of reminder calls; the programme will only make some non-adherent members adherent, so the effect of the programme is smaller than the effect of adherence.
What we did: Stated in the report; the result is the effect of adherence itself, an upper guide for the programme.
Suspected, not in the data: Health-conscious habitsgeneral knowledge, not confirmed
Healthy-adherer effect: people who stick to their pills also look after their health in ways the claims data do not record (diet, exercise, alcohol, keeping appointments).

Meet the methods

These methods come from three generations of thinking about cause and effect. Older ones are simpler and more transparent; newer ones handle messier patterns. Using several side by side shows whether the answer depends on the technique.

Classical statisticsMachine learning with statistical guaranteesYear = when the idea was first published
1800s to 1980s

Classical statistics

Fit a simple formula, then compare like with like. Transparent and fast.

Linear regression. Adjusts for each trait assuming straight-line relationships.
Propensity weighting. Estimates each unit's chance of getting the action, then reweights so the groups look alike.
Watch out: wrong when relationships are curved; weighting gets shaky when some units were near-certain to get the action.
1986 to 2018

Machine learning with statistical guarantees

Flexible machine-learning models learn the patterns; a statistical recipe corrects their bias and gives honest ranges. Today's standard in industry and research.

Doubly robust ML (main). Models both who gets the action and the outcome; still right if either model is.
Double ML (overlap-weighted). Strips out what the traits already predict, then compares what's left. Its average leans towards units that could have gone either way.
Causal forest. Thousands of decision trees that look for who benefits most.
Doubly robust, spline models. The same recipe with smooth-curve models instead of trees.
ML regression. Predicts each unit's outcome with and without the action.
Watch out: need a few thousand rows to shine, and like every method here, can't see factors missing from the data.

How strong would a hidden factor need to be?

Each bar is how strongly a recorded factor drives both the action and the outcome. A hidden factor past the dashed line could erase the effect.

On paper a hidden factor would need to be about 5 times stronger than prior flu vaccination, our strongest recorded driver, to erase this result. That is not reassuring here: flu shots capture only a sliver of 'looks after their health', and the injury check shows the unrecorded part is real.

The action seems to ‘change’ something it can’t

Changes relative to the untreated group’s average. We ran the main method on outcomes the action cannot plausibly affect. They should sit on the no-effect line.

If that hidden difference also inflates the main answer, the effect would be nearer −3.4 per 100 to −0.9 per 100 (middle value −2.2 per 100). That assumes the difference shifts the main outcome by half to one and a half times the share it shifts the check outcome: a sensitivity band, not a corrected answer.

Rough bias correction (custom analysis, outside the tested toolkit)
Assuming hidden habits cut cardiac and injury admissions by the same share, dividing out the injury 'effect' leaves about 2.2 fewer cardiac admissions per 100 (roughly 17% fewer), with a 95% range from about 5.1 fewer to 1.1 more. If the hidden habits cut cardiac admissions by half to one and a half times as much as injuries, the effect would be about 0.9 to 3.4 fewer per 100: a sensitivity band, not a corrected answer. That matches the 20-30% relative reduction seen in statin trials. The equal-bias assumption is itself unproven; habits like diet and exercise may matter more for the heart than for injuries, which would make the true effect smaller still. What to do next

1
Put 2 per 100 in the business case, with 4.5 as the ceiling. About 2 fewer cardiac admissions per 100 fully adherent members a year is the defensible planning figure; 4.5 is the upper end if none of the gap were habits. Also remember the programme will only turn some non-adherent members into adherent ones, so multiply by the share it converts (for example, if calls convert 1 in 4, expect roughly 0.5 fewer admissions per 100 members enrolled).
2
Settle it with a randomised roll-out. Offer the reminder calls to a random half of new statin starters and track both adherence and admissions. Spotting a 2-point drop in cardiac admissions needs about 4,400 members per group; a 1-point drop needs about 18,200 per group. The random offer also lets you measure the effect of adherence among the members the calls actually moved.
3
Start with members with diabetes. They show the largest estimated gain, so a pilot there gives the best chance of seeing a measurable effect.

Questions for you

We assumed the column meanings in your brief are right. In particular that 'adherent' means at least 80% of days covered in months 1-6, and that admissions are counted only in months 7-18.
Were members who died or left the plan in the first 6 months dropped from the file? If so, the file may exclude the sickest early stoppers, which could bias the comparison either way. We assumed the file covers everyone who reached month 7.
Is 'one number for adherence' the right number for Finance? We answered the effect of being adherent versus not, averaged over all members. The programme's effect is that figure times the share of members the calls convert, and may be lower if the easiest-to-convert members are the healthier ones.
Do you have other health-seeking markers? Screening uptake, missed appointments, or other preventive fills would strengthen the healthy-adherer correction. We treated health-conscious habits as a suspected unrecorded driver (general knowledge, not confirmed by you).
