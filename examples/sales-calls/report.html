About the methods above

Meet the methods

These methods come from three generations of thinking about cause and effect. Older ones are simpler and more transparent; newer ones handle messier patterns. Using several side by side shows whether the answer depends on the technique.

Classical statisticsMachine learning with statistical guaranteesFoundation models (transformers)Year = when the idea was first published
1800s to 1980s

Classical statistics

Fit a simple formula, then compare like with like. Transparent and fast.

Linear regression. Adjusts for each trait assuming straight-line relationships.
Propensity weighting. Estimates each unit's chance of getting the action, then reweights so the groups look alike.
Watch out: wrong when relationships are curved; weighting gets shaky when some units were near-certain to get the action.
1986 to 2018

Machine learning with statistical guarantees

Flexible machine-learning models learn the patterns; a statistical recipe corrects their bias and gives honest ranges. Today's standard in industry and research.

Doubly robust ML. Models both who gets the action and the outcome; still right if either model is.
Double ML. Strips out what the traits already predict, then compares what's left.
Causal forest. Thousands of decision trees that look for who benefits most.
Doubly robust, spline models. The same recipe with smooth-curve models instead of trees.
ML regression. Predicts each unit's outcome with and without the action.
Watch out: need a few thousand rows to shine, and like every method here, can't see factors missing from the data.
2023 to today

Foundation models

Transformers, the architecture behind large language models, pre-trained on millions of simulated datasets. They recognise the pattern in your data without being trained on it.

CausalPFN. Built for cause and effect: reads the data and estimates the effect in one pass.
Watch out: the newest and least proven. Accurate in tests, but their own ranges run too narrow, so they are cross-checks only.

What the data can still tell us

With no assumptions at all the range is too wide to be useful. With two plain assumptions (a call never makes an account less likely to buy, and reps pick accounts that would have bought at least as often without a call) the effect is between 0 and about 25 extra purchases per 100 calls. For a hiring decision, the lower part of that range is the safer planning figure: new reps would be calling accounts that current reps chose not to call, and those are probably less ready to buy.

What we can say without more assumptions

With no assumptions: between −33.4 per 100 and 66.6 per 100.

If the action never hurts, and those who got it would have done at least as well anyway: between 0.0 per 100 and 24.9 per 100.

Why the grade is D

The checks we can run pass: called and uncalled accounts look alike on the recorded traits after adjustment, and a fake, randomly shuffled call showed no effect, as it should. None of that can detect the reps' unrecorded judgement, which you told us drives who gets called.

How strong would a hidden factor need to be?

Each bar is how strongly a recorded factor drives both the action and the outcome. A hidden factor past the dashed line could erase the effect.

A hidden factor would need to be about 31 times as strong as last year's purchases to erase this. That sounds reassuring but isn't: the reps' gut feel about who is ready to buy could easily be that strong, because our recorded traits are weak predictors of both calls and purchases.

What this rests on

Arrows mean "affects". Drawn from your brief: reps pick 'warm' accounts, and that judgement is not recorded. Not yet confirmed with you.
Calls and purchases overlap in time
Calls are logged 'during the quarter' and purchases 'within 90 days of the quarter start'. Some calls may have come after the purchase (for example an onboarding or thank-you call). Those would inflate the apparent link. If call and purchase dates exist, we can keep only calls made before any purchase.

What to do next

1
Run a short randomized call test before hiring. Take accounts reps would not normally call and randomly split them: half get a call, half don't. About 862 accounts per group spots a lift of 5 purchases per 100 calls; about 238 per group spots 10 per 100. This measures exactly what new reps would add.
2
Start recording the reps' 'warm' judgement. A simple warm/cold tag logged before each call would let future analyses separate the rep's pick from the call itself.
3
Plan hiring on the low end for now. Until a test is done, treat 25 extra purchases per 100 calls as a ceiling, not an expectation.
